\title{Simulating the flow of viscous, incompressible fluids with a free surface using terrain-following coordinates}
\author{Daniel R. Shapero, ...TBD}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\abstract{This paper describes how to simulate viscous thin-film fluid flows using a combination of terrain-following coordinates and discontinuous Galerkin discretization.
Terrain-following coordinates were originally developed for simulating the flow of the atmosphere and ocean over variable topography.
For these problems, viscosity is negligible, so the equations of motion contain only first-order derivatives in space and time.
Here we are interested in free-surface flows where viscous effects are dominant.
These problems include the flow of glaciers, lava, or the earth's mantle.
The equations of motion now contain second-order derivatives when viscosity is important.
Transforming these terms to the new coordinate system is more complex.
In particular, the warping factor from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates depends on the slope of the surface and bed.
When the topography has been discretized in a finite element space, these slopes jump across cell boundaries.
This discontinuity appears at first to make it impossible to discretize the problem.
Our main contribution is to show how this challenge can be overcome using discontinuous Galerkin methods.}


% --------------------
\section{Introduction}

Our goal in this paper is to describe techniques for simulating the flow of thin fluid films over variable topography at low Reynolds number with applications to glaciology and geodynamics.
When the surface of the fluid is free to move up and down, the geometry of the problem is changing in time.
Simulations in an evolving geometry are harder than in a fixed geometry.
In atmospheric science and oceanography, a common remedy is to use \emph{terrain-following coordinates} \citep{gal1975use}.
This coordinate system uses a non-dimensional vertical coordinate $\zeta$ which takes values between 0 and 1.
The physical elevation in Cartesian coordinates is
\begin{equation}
    z = b(x, y) + \zeta \cdot h(x, y, t)
\end{equation}
where $b$ and $h$ are the bottom topography and fluid thickness.
See Figure \ref{fig:tfc-vs-xyz} for an illustration.
We assume that the fluid film thickness can change in time but that the bottom topography is fixed.
When using Cartesian coordinates, the computational grid has to be deformed to the surface and bottom topography.
This results in more complicated formulas to discretize a conservation law.
In terrain-following coordinates, the grid is regular, but now there are warping terms in the equations of motion.

A running theme throughout the following will be what we mean by ``velocity''.
The velocity is the rate of change of the position of a particle that is moving with the fluid.
How we represent that position, and thus the velocity, depends on the coordinate system.
For example, suppose we follow a material parcel at the bottom of a fluid film.
We assume that the fluid is not in perfect contact with its bed, so it can slip but it cannot penetrate through the bed.
This assumption means that the velocity vector is always parallel to the bed.
In Cartesian coordinates, the vertical component of the velocity will be positive when the particle is flowing uphill and negative when the particle is flowing downhill.
But the vertical component of the velocity will always be zero in terrain-following coordinates.

Now there is a choice to make: do we solve for the Cartesian components of the velocity vector, or do we solve for the intrinsic, contravariant components of the velocity vector in the transformed coordinate system?
In this work, we will always solve for the intrinsic, contravariant components of the velocity vector in terrain-following coordinates.
We argue that this has certain conceptual advantages.
But we emphasize that the representation is a modeling choice.

Several papers use Riemannian geometry to describe the equations of motion in general coordinate systems.
The seminal paper of \citet{gal1975use} adopts this approach.
This viewpoint replaces the Euclidean inner product with a metric tensor $g_{ij}(x)$.
The metric tensor tells us how to calculate inner products between direction vectors at the point $x$.
The metric tensor has to be symmetric and positive definite.
Unlike the Euclidean inner product, it can vary in space.
We can then compute the Christoffel (``Christ-awful'') symbols for this metric, which allows us to derive expressions for the gradient, divergence, and curl.

There is a major problem with adopting Riemannian geometry when using the finite element method.
The metric tensor depends on the slope of the bed and surface.
But our goal was to describe fluid films with a free surface.
The free surface evolution law is a hyperbolic partial differential equation.
Suppose we discretize this equation using continuous Galerkin basis functions.
Then the slope field jumps at cell boundaries, and as a consequence the metric tensor does too.
The gradient and divergence operators in curvilinear coordinates involve the Christoffel symbols, which are defined as certain combinations of derivatives of the metric tensor.
But the metric tensor is not even continuous, much less differentiable.
Using terrain-following coordinates and finite elements might then appear doomed.

The alternative approach that we adopt here is to use a curvilinear coordinate system while avoiding Riemannian geometry.
Instead of working with the metric tensor, we rely on the linearization $J$ of the map from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates and its inverse.
The matrix $J$ is also discontinuous across cell boundaries, and it appears under a gradient operator.
At first blush, it might seem that this approach too is doomed to fail for the same reason as using Riemannian geometry.
In this case, however, we can salvage the wreck using discontinuous Galerkin methods.

The orthodox approach to simulating free-surface fluid flow in both glaciology and geodynamics is to use a Cartesian coordinate system and a moving mesh.
It is common to use operator-splitting or semi-implicitization schemes to separate the evolution of the free surface from the solution of the momentum balance equation \citep{gagliardini2013capabilities, rose2017stability}.
Operator-splitting schemes can require small timesteps in order to avoid the ``drunken sailor'' instability.
How to stabilize the numerical scheme is an active area of research \citep{ahlkrona2026fully}.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
Terrain-following coordinates are pervasive in atmospheric science and oceanography \citep{gal1975use}.
Several papers have examined how to rewrite the Navier-Stokes equations in curvilinear coordinate systems using Riemannian geometry \citep{luo2004contravariant}.
A handful of papers in aeronautical engineering have derived the Navier-Stokes equations in curvilinear coordinate systems using only the linearization map $J$ instead of a metric tensor \citep{warsi1981conservation, yang1994general}.
\citet{cannata2019numerical} used these techniques to discretize the equations of free surface fluid flow, but they used a finite volume and not a finite element discretization.
\citet{greve2009dynamics} use terrain-following coordinates for glacier flow, but their approach differs from ours in two ways.
First, they do not use the Stokes equations but rather a perturbative simplification based on the smallness of the domain aspect ratio.
Second, they work with the Cartesian components of the velocity vector rather than the contravariant components in the transformed coordinate system.

Our contribution is to show how to discretize thin-film flow at low Reynolds number using discontinuous Galerkin methods.
This is summed up in its entirety in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc}, which is the most important part of the paper.
To our knowledge, this equation has not appeared in the glaciology or geodynamics literature before.
An important consequence is that we can solve the coupled mass and momentum balance equations all at once without recourse to operator splitting.

We lean heavily on discontinuous Galerkin methods in this paper.
These schemes are very well-studied in the applied mathematics literature.
But their broad diffusion into other disciplines in science and engineering is still uneven.
For this reason, we aim to cover the details at a lower level than is strictly necessary.
We refer to \citet{di2011mathematical} for further reading.

\section{Background}

Here we review the Stokes equations in Cartesian coordinates and how to discretize them with interior penalty methods.
The interior penalty method will prove indispensible when we switch from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates.

\subsection{Notation}

The spatial domain will always be denoted $\Omega$.
The main field to be solved for is the velocity vector $u$.
When we have to specify whether we are working with the components of the velocity vector in Cartesian vs. terrain-following coordinates, we will write this as $u_x$ and $u_\xi$ respectively.
We will drop the subscripts where it should be clear from context.
A generic cell of the computational mesh that discretizes the domain will be written as $\omega$ and a facet of a cell as $\gamma$.
Likewise, we will write the elements of volume and surface area as $\mathrm d\omega$ and $\mathrm d\gamma$ respectively.
When we need to distinguish between the element of volume in the Cartesian or the terrain-following coordinate frame, we will write these as $\mathrm d\omega_x$, $\mathrm d\omega_\xi$ and likewise for area.

We will write the norm of a vector $v$ as $|v|$ and likewise the norm of a rank-2 tensor.
We will denote the inner product between two tensors with a colon, i.e. given two tensors $A$, $B$,
\begin{equation}
    A : B = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}.
\end{equation}
We will also denote the volume of a cell $\omega$ as $|\omega|$ and likewise the area of a facet.

When integrating over a facet $\gamma$ in the interior of the mesh, we often need to refer to averages of some scalar, vector, or tensor quantity across both of the cells $\omega_+$, $\omega_-$ that contain $\gamma$.
Given some field $\phi$, we denote $\phi_+$, $\phi_-$ as the limiting values of $\phi$ as it approaches the facet $\gamma$ from the $\omega_+$ and $\omega_-$ sides respectively.
We do not anticipate that $\phi$ is continuous and so these limiting values can be different.
We use angle brackets to denote an average across a facet:
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi\rangle = \frac{\phi_+ + \phi_-}{2}.
\end{equation}
To express DG methods, we will also need the jump of some quantity across a facet.
The jump is written with square brackets.
The jump of a scalar or vector quantity can be defined as the difference in values:
\begin{equation}
    [\phi] = \phi_+ - \phi_-.
\end{equation}
But we will also need a jump that is weighted by the outward-pointing normal vector $n$.
Note that the outward-pointing normal vector to $\omega_+$ has the opposite sign as the outward-pointing normal to its neighboring cell $n_-$.
In other words, $n_- = -n_+$.
The jump of a term weighted by the normal vector is
\begin{equation}
    [\phi\otimes n] = \phi_+\otimes n_+ + \phi_-\otimes n_-
\end{equation}
where $\otimes$ denotes the outer product of tensors.
In other words, the normal-weighted jump is always one rank higher than its argument (a scalar maps to a vector, a vector to a tensor, etc.).
Note how we add the two terms instead of subtracting them because the sign flip is implicit in the normal vector.

The discrete form of the problem using a DG basis contains sums of integrals over facets of the mesh.
Some of these terms contain integrals over only certain types of facets.
For example, one of the integrals we need to calculate is evaluated only over the part of the domain boundary where Dirichlet conditions are applied, and another for Robin conditions.
Additionally, there are some simplifications to the algebra that we can make by separately evaluating integrals over horizontal and vertical facets.
We will write $\Gamma$ as the collection of all facets of the mesh, and a subscript to specify which subset of facets to sum over.
So to sum over only the facets $\gamma$ that are on the domain boundary where Dirichlet conditions are applied, we will write
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{\gamma \in \Gamma_D}\int_\gamma\big\{\cdots\big\}\mathrm d\gamma.
\end{equation}
The relevant subsets are the set of Dirichlet facets $\Gamma_D$ and Robin facets $\Gamma_R$ on the domain boundary, and the horizontal and vertical interior facets $\Gamma_H$ and $\Gamma_V$.


\subsection{The Stokes equations}

The incompressible Stokes equations can be derived through minimization of the free energy dissipation rate
\begin{equation}
    G(u, p) = \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau :\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot u - f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm d\omega
\end{equation}
where $\dot\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla u + \nabla u^*)$ is the rate of strain tensor and $\tau = 2\mu\dot\varepsilon$ is the deviatoric stress tensor.
We will use the minimization form in the following because it requires fewer characters to type.

The description above requires boundary conditions to be complete.
The most common boundary condition is to fix the the velocity to some externally-imposed value $u_\Gamma$, i.e. a Dirichlet boundary condition.
The conventional way to impose Dirichlet boundary conditions is to change rows of the system matrix and right-hand side in order to force the boundary degrees of freedom to take the desired boundary values.
We can also fix the normal stress at the boundary (a Neumann boundary condition) by adding another term to the variational form.
For example, the top surface of the fluid is usually assumed to be stress-free, in which case we do nothing at all.
For some fluid flow problems we want to prescribe the friction along the boundary.
Friction boundary conditions are a mixture of two different types in the normal and tangential directions.
The velocity in the normal direction is set to some fixed value $\dot m$, which is usually but not always zero:
\begin{equation}
    u\cdot n = \dot m
    \label{eq:normal-flow-bc}
\end{equation}
In the tangential direction, the traction is proportional to the slip velocity:
\begin{equation}
    (I - n\otimes n)\tau\,n = -\kappa(I - n\otimes n)u.
\end{equation}
Here the matrix $I - n\otimes n$ projects a vector onto the plane tangent to the surface.

\subsection{Discontinuous Galerkin discretization}

\subsubsection{Nitsche's method}

The conventional way to enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions is to modify the discretized linear system.
\emph{Nitsche's method} is a way to enforce boundary conditions by instead modifying the variational form \citep{nitsche1971variationsprinzip}.
Here we give a brief overview of Nitsche's method for two reasons.
First, it leads in a natural way to discontinuous Galerkin methods.
Second, we will need Nitsche's method in order to apply slip boundary conditions.

A blunt way to enforce boundary conditions is to add a penalty on any departure of $u$ from $u_\Gamma$ along the boundary to the functional to be minimized.
The penalty method is not used often in practice because it makes the condition number growth of the linear system much worse.
Nitsche's method amounts to applying a more sophisticated technique than the penalty method together with some special characteristics of finite element discretizations \citep{stenberg1995some}.
The Nitsche functional is
\begin{equation}
    G_\alpha(u, p) = G(u, p) - \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_D}\int_\gamma(\tau - pI) : n\otimes(u - u_\Gamma)\mathrm d\gamma + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_D}\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu}{2\lambda}|u - u_\Gamma|^2\mathrm d\gamma.
    \label{eq:nitsche-dirichlet}
\end{equation}
The integrals are again over all mesh facets that are contained in the segment $\Gamma_D$ of the domain boundary where the velocity should be fixed to $u_\Gamma$.
The second term, which we refer to as the facet flux, is what makes Nitsche's method work better than the penalty method.
Here $\lambda$ is a measure of the local facet diameter.
In the following, we will take $\lambda$ to be equal to the volume-to-surface ratio $|\omega|/|\gamma|$ of cell containing $\gamma$.
This choice works especially better for anisotropic meshes than, for example, the diameter of $\gamma$.
The constant $\alpha$ is dimensionless and can be chosen explicitly based on the problem dimension and the degree of the finite element basis \citep{warburton2003constants}.
Finding a minimizer of the functional in equation \eqref{eq:nitsche-dirichlet} solves the original problem and boundary conditions up to discretization error without having to modify the linear system.

So Nitsche's method offers another way to enforce Dirichlet boundary conditions, but one could argue what value it brings since we can always modify the linear system.
For slip boundary conditions, on the other hand, Nitsche's method is the \emph{only} principled solution.
It is not possible to satisfy the normal-flow condition \eqref{eq:normal-flow-bc} exactly when using continuous Galerkin finite elements for the velocity on a curved surface.
The normal vector is defined on facets but is not well-defined at mesh vertices.
Meanwhile, velocity has degrees of freedom at the vertices for most of the common finite element bases that are used in practice.
So the conventional approach of modifying the linear system is not available for this type of boundary condition.
We can instead use Nitsche's method to enforce the normal-flow condition approximately:
\begin{equation}
    G_\alpha(u, p) = G(u, p) - \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma n\cdot(\tau - pI)n\,(u\cdot n)\mathrm d\gamma + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu}{2\lambda}(u\cdot n)^2\mathrm d\gamma + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\kappa}{2}|(I - n\otimes n)u|^2\mathrm d\gamma
    \label{eq:nitsche-slip}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the friction coefficient \citep{freund1995weakly, cheng2020full}.
The flux term contains the full stress for Dirichlet conditions, but only the normal-normal component of the stress for Robin conditions.
Likewise the penalty for Dirichlet conditions is on the square norm of $u - u_\Gamma$, while the penalty for Robin conditions is the square norm of $u\cdot n$.

\subsubsection{Interior penalty methods}

The discussion above assumes that we are using conventional finite element bases, which are continuous across cell boundaries.
When we switch to terrain-following coordinates, we will have to use a scheme that works even when the basis functions are not continuous.
The typical reason to work with the more flexible class of discontinuous basis functions is to solve advection-dominated problems.
The problems we focus on here are not advection-dominated.
All the same, these \emph{discontinuous Galerkin} (DG) methods will, as we show below, solve one of the thornier issues that we have to confront.
To be consistent with the ideal problem, a DG method has to recover continuity in the limit as the mesh is refined.
The \emph{symmetric interior penalty} discontinuous Galerkin (SIPDG) method is one type of scheme that provably achieves this goal.

There are several ways to derive SIPDG schemes.
One approach is to apply Nitsche's method to every cell $\omega$ of the mesh.
Where Nitsche's method was originally created in order to impose Dirichlet boundary conditions on the outer boundary of the whole domain, the SIPDG method instead uses the same idea but to enforce continuity.
Leaving aside any boundary conditions for now, the objective functional we extremize is:
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz}
    \begin{align}
        G_\alpha(u, p) = & \sum_\omega\int_\omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau:\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot u - f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm d\omega \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz-cells} \\
        & \qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma \left\langle\tau - pI \right\rangle: [u\otimes n]\,\mathrm d\gamma \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz-flux} \\
        & \qquad\qquad + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu|\gamma|}{2|\omega|}|[u\otimes n]|^2\mathrm d\gamma. \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz-penalty}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here we have explicitly written the length scale in the penalty term as the volume-to-surface ratio.
The other distinguishing feature to notice is in the penalty term.
Here we've used $|[u\otimes n]|^2$ as the penalty.
This expression is mathematically equal to $|[u]|^2$, which is simpler.
We've included the factor of the normal vector because it makes equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz-penalty} transform in a more favorable way in curvilinear coordinates.
It also generalizes more naturally to anisotropic viscous fluids like liquid crystals or to nonlinear rheologies.

Figure \ref{fig:source-code-xyz} shows the source code for producing the free energy rate in Cartesian coordinates for both conforming and non-conforming elements.
To add Dirichlet or slip boundary conditions, we add the same extra boundary integrals onto the end of equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz} as in equations \eqref{eq:nitsche-dirichlet} and \eqref{eq:nitsche-slip} respectively.

Here we've describe the IPDG method as an application of Nitsche's method, but we can arrive at the same scheme in more than one way.
For example, \citet{cockburn2002local} derive interior penalty methods as a conservation law with a particular choice of numerical flux.

\section{Terrain-following coordinates}

The geometry of a free-surface flow is changing in time.
There are choices the modeler has to make about how to capture this effect and no one choice is clearly superior.
One possibility is to use a fixed coordinate system and a moving mesh.
The ice-flow modeling pacakge Elmer/ICE uses a Cartesian coordinate system and advects the mesh with the flow using an arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian scheme \citep{gagliardini2013capabilities}.
Alternatively, one can use a fixed mesh and a moving coordinate system.
The package SICOPOLIS uses terrain-following coordinates, described in \S5.7 of \citet{greve2009dynamics} and below.
Finally, one can use a completely fixed mesh and coordinate system and use interface-tracking techniques such as the level-set or volume-of-fluid methods \citep{hossain2020modelling, gray2026variational}.
\textcolor{red}{Check ISSM, PISM, BISICLES if we want to be exhaustive}.
\textcolor{red}{This text is also very glaciology-biased and we should talk about geodynamics a bit.}
Here we will pursue the approach of using a coordinate system that is fixed in the horizontal but moving in the vertical.

We can solve the problem of a moving geometry by transforming the coordinates.
The idea of terrain-following coordinates is to map the lower surface to 0 and the upper surface to 1.
In so doing, we can work on the Cartesian product of a footprint domain and the unit interval $[0, 1]$.
We will denote the Cartesian coordinates as $x_1, x_2, x_3$ and terrain-following coordinate as $\xi_1, \xi_2, \xi_3$.
The transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates is
\begin{equation}
    \left[\begin{matrix}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3\end{matrix}\right] = \left[\begin{matrix}\xi_1 \\ \xi_2 \\ b(\xi_1, \xi_2) + \xi_3h(\xi_1, \xi_2)\end{matrix}\right].
    \label{eq:explicit-tfc}
\end{equation}
In this coordinate system, we get to pretend like the domain is a slab with a constant thickness that doesn't change in time.

This coordinate transformation solves some problems only to create others.
Part of the reason that Cartesian (and cylindrical and spherical polar) coordinates are convenient is because the coordinate surfaces are perpendicular to each other.
In terrain-following coordinates, the physical image of the surface of constant $\xi_3$ is curved.
Taking a derivative with respect to the horizontal coordinates with the elevation $x_3$ fixed is not the same as taking a derivative with $\xi_3$ fixed.
But the surfaces of constant $\xi_3$ are also not orthogonal to the surfaces of constant $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$ and so we end up with more complicated expressions for vectors and gradients.
To use this new coordinate system, we have to systematically transform every term in the Stokes equations.
We show all the transformation rules and the transformed Stokes equations in the next subsections.

All of the transformation rules described below are summarized in Table \ref{tab:xform-rules}.


\subsection{Transformation rules}

The explicit expression in equation \eqref{eq:explicit-tfc} for the transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates will let us also transform vector and tensor fields and their derivatives.
First, we'll introduce a vector field
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \nabla b + \xi_3\nabla h
    \label{eq:slope-field}
\end{equation}
describing the slope in physical space of surfaces of constant $\xi_3$.
The vector field $\sigma$ interpolates between the slope of the bed and the slope of the surface.
In the expression for the slope field, we don't have to specify whether we are taking the gradient in one coordinate system or the other because $b$ and $h$ only depend on the horizontal coordinates.
Then we can concisely express the derivative of the transformation from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates as
\begin{equation}
    J = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\xi} = \left[\begin{matrix}I & 0 \\ \sigma^* & h\end{matrix}\right].
    \label{eq:dx-dxi}
\end{equation}
All that the transformation rules below require is an explicit expression for $J$ or its inverse.
The derivations are all rudimentary multivariable calculus.
Nonetheless implementating the total of all of them can be error-prone, so they are worth repeating.

\paragraph{Volumes.}
Transforming volume integrals follows the familiar rule of multiplying by the determinant of the linearization of the transformation map:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm d\omega_x = |\det J|\ud\omega_\xi = h\ud\omega_\xi.
    \label{eq:xform-volume}
\end{equation}
As a sanity check, this weighting factor makes the physical units work out correctly.

\paragraph{Velocity vectors.}
We also have to think about how to transform vectors and tensors between coordinate systems.
The velocity vector itself has an existence independent of the frame of reference.
But the components of the velocity in Cartesian ($u_x$) and terrain-following ($u_\xi$) coordinates are not the same.
We can derive the relation between them using the chain rule, and reasoning informally that a velocity is a rate of change of position with time:
\begin{equation}
    u_x = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\xi}\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dt} = Ju_\xi.
    \label{eq:xform-velocity}
\end{equation}
The body forces transform the same way as well:
\begin{equation}
    f_x = Jf_\xi.
\end{equation}
Intuitively, the gravitational acceleration vector is perpendicular to surfaces of constant depth but is not perpendicular to surfaces of constant relative depth.
We can see from the explicit expression for $J$ in equation \eqref{eq:dx-dxi} that, because of the identity block, the only difference is in the vertical components of the velocity and force.

\paragraph{Gradients.}
Next we need to be able to transform derivatives.
To do that, we'll need to know what the derivative of the inverse transformation back from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates is.
We could compute the inverse transformation and then differentiate it, or we could use the implicit function theorem and the fact that $J$ is a triangular matrix.
These yield the explicit expression
\begin{equation}
    J^{-1} = \frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx} = \left[\begin{matrix}I & 0 \\ -h^{-1}\sigma^* & h^{-1}\end{matrix}\right].
    \label{eq:dxi-dx}
\end{equation}
Given an arbitrary field $\phi$, the chain rule tells us that
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dx} = \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm d\xi}\cdot\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx} = \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm d\xi}\cdot J^{-1}.
    \label{eq:xform-gradient}
\end{equation}
This is just a restatement of the fact that derivatives transform as covectors and not as vectors.
We can then write down an expression for the velocity gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_xu_x = \nabla_\xi(Ju_\xi)J^{-1}.
    \label{eq:velocity-gradient}
\end{equation}
We get the strain rate tensor by symmetrizing the above expression.

\paragraph{Normals.}
The unit outward normal ``vector'' to a volume element is not really a vector; it's a covector.
It does not transform by multiplication on the left by $J$.
A heuristic that can help to remember the right transformation rule is to think of the outward-pointing normal vector as the gradient of the signed distance function to the interior of the domain.
This heuristic makes it clear that (1) the outward-pointing normal is not a column vector but a row vector and (2) it transforms by picking up a factor of $J^{-1}$ on the right.
In short,
\begin{equation}
    n_x = \frac{n_\xi\,J^{-1}}{|n_\xi\,J^{-1}|}.
    \label{eq:xform-normal}
\end{equation}
Here there is a normalization factor in the denominator in order to ensure that $n_x$ has unit length.
In the integrals we write down to express the Stokes problem, often this normalization factor in the denominator will cancel with the same factor in the numerator of a different expression.

The formula in equation \eqref{eq:xform-normal} works in any curvilinear coordinate system.
There are a few particular features of terrain-following coordinates that simplify the algebra.
The computational domain is the Cartesian product of a 2D footprint and the unit interval, so the normal vectors $n_\xi$ are always either purely horizontal or vertical.
On vertical facets, the normal vectors in Cartesian and terrain-following coordinates are the same.
Applying equations \eqref{eq:xform-normal} and \eqref{eq:dxi-dx} to horizontal facets results in a cancellation of a factor of $h$ in both the numerator and denominator.
Putting the two cases together, we get
\begin{equation}
    n_x = \begin{cases}n_\xi & \gamma\in\Gamma_V \\ \frac{(-\sigma^*, 1)}{\sqrt{1 + |\sigma|^2}} & \gamma\in\Gamma_H \end{cases}
    \label{eq:xform-normal-tfc}
\end{equation}
where again $\Gamma_V$ are the vertical facets and $\Gamma_H$ the horizontal facets.

\paragraph{Area elements.}
While volume elements all transform by multiplication by a factor of $|\det J|$, surface area elements transform differently depending on their orientation.
The general rule is
\begin{equation}
    d\gamma_x = |\det J|\cdot |n_\xi\,J^{-1}|\,d\gamma_\xi.
    \label{eq:xform-area}
\end{equation}
We can simplify this general formula knowing that facets will always be either horizontal or vertical:
\begin{equation}
    |\det J|\cdot|n_\xi J^{-1}| = \begin{cases} h & \gamma \in \Gamma_V \\ \sqrt{1 + |\sigma|^2} & \gamma \in \Gamma_H \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where once again a factor of $h$ in the numerator and the denominator cancel out for the horizontal facets.
Like the transformation rule for the element of volume, we can also sanity-check this expression by checking the physical units in each case.

Most of the facet integrals that we need to calculate include a factor of the normal vector.
On the horizontal facets, the factor of $\sqrt{1 + |\sigma|^2}$ in the last equation will then cancel out with its reciprocal in equation \eqref{eq:xform-normal-tfc}.

\paragraph{Length scales.}
Interior penalty methods require some length scale in order to penalize jumps across facets.
In equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz} we used the volume-to-surface ratio to set this length scale:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_x = \frac{|\omega_x|}{|\gamma_x|}
\end{equation}
where $|\omega_x|$, $|\gamma_x|$ are the volume and surface area of the cell and facet in Cartesian coordinates.
This ratio gives a measure of the cell size normal to the face $\gamma$ and tends to work better on anisotropic meshes.
We can define the transformed length scale by combining the transformation rules for volumes and elements.
Here a factor of $|\det J|$ in the numerator and denominator cancel:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_x = \frac{\cancel{|\det J|}|\omega_\xi|}{\cancel{|\det J|}|n_\xi\,J^{-1}||\gamma_\xi|} = \frac{|\omega_\xi|}{|n_\xi\,J^{-1}||\gamma_\xi|}
    \label{eq:xform-length-scale}
\end{equation}
This equation likewise simplifies by case analysis on the facet orientation:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_x = \frac{|\omega_\xi|}{|\gamma_\xi|}\cdot \begin{cases}1 & \gamma\in\Gamma_V \\ \frac{h}{\sqrt{1 + |\sigma|^2}} & \gamma\in\Gamma_H\end{cases}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Divergences.}
The divergence of a vector field is more involved.
In Cartesian coordinates, the divergence operator is the negative adjoint of the gradient operator:
\begin{equation}
    \int_\Omega q\nabla_x \cdot v_x\,\mathrm d\omega_x = -\int_\Omega\nabla_x q\cdot v_x\,\mathrm d\omega_x + \ldots
\end{equation}
Here we ignore irrelevant boundary integrals that arise from using integration by parts.
The general expression for the divergence in other coordinate systems follows by requiring this relationship to remain true.
We know how $\mathrm d\omega$, $v_x$, and $\nabla_x q$ transform from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates:
\begin{align}
    \ldots & = -\int_\Omega\nabla_\xi q\cdot J^{-1}\cdot Ju_\xi\;h\ud\omega_\xi \nonumber\\
    & = -\int_\Omega\nabla_\xi q\cdot h u_\xi\ud\omega_\xi \nonumber\\
    & = +\int_\Omega q\nabla_\xi\cdot(hu_\xi)\ud\omega_\xi.
\end{align}
Here we used the fact that the factors of $J^{-1}$ and $J$ cancel out, then applied the usual trick to move the gradient back over as a divergence.
Now we can assert that defining
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\cdot u_x = h^{-1}\nabla_\xi\cdot (hu_\xi)
    \label{eq:xform-divergence}
\end{equation}
preserves the relationship between the divergence of a vector field and the gradient of a scalar field.


\subsection{The Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates}

We can proceed by systematically applying the transformation rules, summarized in Table \ref{tab:xform-rules}, to the minimization form of the Stokes problem.
We will start with the objective for the original problem.
The DG facet terms are shown separately below.
The objective becomes
\begin{equation}
    G(u, p) = \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}h\tau :\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot(hu) - hf\cdot J^*J u\right\}\mathrm d\omega
    \label{eq:stokes-tfc}
\end{equation}
where the deviatoric stress $\tau$ is again equal to $2\mu\dot\varepsilon$, but the rate of strain tensor is
\begin{equation}
    \dot\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left\{\nabla(Ju)J^{-1} + J^{-*}\nabla (Ju)^*\right\}.
    \label{eq:strain-rate-tfc}
\end{equation}
The integrand, provided that we express it in terms of the strain rate, is largely similar to the expression in Cartesian coordinates but for a few extra factors of $h$.
The extra terms are all expressible in the Unified Form Language or related domain-specific languages for PDEs.

Finally, the thickness evolves in time according to a conservative advection equation.
The variational form of this equation is
\begin{equation}
    \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\phi - hu\cdot\nabla\phi\right\}\mathrm d\omega = \int_\Omega(\dot a - \dot m)\phi\ud\omega
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is an arbitrary test function and $\dot a$, $\dot m$ are the accumulation and ablation rates.
The usual form of this equation as described in e.g. \citet{greve2009dynamics} instead uses the depth-averaged velocity $\bar u$, and the integration domain is the lower-dimensional footprint of the computational mesh.
We can instead use a finite element space for the thickness $h$ which is defined on the entire domain.
The finite element for $h$ is the tensor product of an ordinary continuous element in the horizontal plane and an element which takes constant values in the vertical dimension.
The derivative w.r.t. the vertical coordinate of any function in this space is zero.
We then recover the depth-average of $u$ by virtue of integrating through the column.
This approach might be less efficient computationally but here we are more concerned with correctness than with performance optimization.

\subsection{Discontinuous Galerkin discretization}

The objective functional in equations \eqref{eq:stokes-tfc} and \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc} describes the solution of the Stokes problem in an ideal curvilinear geometry.
But our goal is to simulate fluid flows with an evolving free surface.
When we discretize the bed elevation and fluid thickness with conventional finite elements, the slope field $\sigma$ in equation \eqref{eq:slope-field} is discontinuous in the horizontal direction.
Consequently, the deformation matrix $J$ is discontinuous, and it appears under a derivative in equation \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc}.
\textbf{The discontinuity of the deformation matrix is the major barrier that we need to overcome.}

def stress_strain_rate(u, b, h):
    J, J_inv = coordinate_transformation_derivatives(b, h)
    Ju = dot(J, u)
    du = dot(grad(Ju), J_inv)
    ε = sym(du)
    return ε, 2 * μ * ε


def free_energy_rate(u, p, f, b, h):
    J, _ = coordinate_transformation_derivatives(b, h)
    Ju = dot(J, u)
    ε, τ = stress_strain_rate(u, b, h)
    return (0.5 * h * inner(τ, ε) - p * div(h * u) - h * inner(f, Ju)) * dx


def free_energy_rate_facets(u, p, b, h):
    J, J_inv = coordinate_transformation_derivatives(b, h)
    n = firedrake.FacetNormal(mesh)
    λ = firedrake.CellVolume(mesh) / firedrake.FacetArea(mesh)

    Ju = dot(J, u)
    ε, τ = stress_strain_rate(u, b, h)

    u_n = outer(Ju, dot(n, J_inv))
    δu = u_n('+') + u_n('-')
    G_stress_flux = -inner(avg(τ), δu) * avg(h) * dS
    G_pressure_flux = avg(p) * jump(h * u, n) * dS
    G_penalty = α * μ / (2 * avg(λ)) * inner(δu, δu) * avg(h) * dS

    return G_stress_flux + G_pressure_flux + G_penalty

We can solve this problem with the interior penalty DG method.
At a practical level, this amounts to applying the transformation rules of Table \ref{tab:xform-rules} to the DG facet corrections.
The analogue of equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz} in terrain-following coordinates is then
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc}
    \begin{align}
        G_\alpha(u, p) & = \sum_\omega\int_\omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}h\tau:\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot(h u) - hf\cdot J^*Ju\right\}\ud\omega \\
        & \qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\left\{\left\langle \tau\right\rangle:[Ju\otimes n\,J^{-1}] + \langle p\rangle[u\cdot n] \right\}h\ud\gamma \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc-flux} \\
        & \quad\qquad + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu|\gamma|}{2|\omega|}|[Ju\otimes nJ^{-1}]|^2\,h\ud\gamma \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc-penalty}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where again $\tau = 2\mu\dot\varepsilon$ and the strain rate tensor in terrain-following coordinates is defined in equation \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc}.
We have simplified the expression above beyond what we would obtain by naively applying all of the transformation rules.
In equations \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc-flux} and \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc-penalty}, we can cancel out factors of $|n\,J^{-1}|$ that appear in both the numerator and the denominator.

Equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc} is the key result of this paper.
To our knowledge it has not appeared before in the literature.
Using either terrain-following coordinates or discontinuous Galerkin methods is not new.
The novelty of this approach is in using DG methods to overcome the continuity obstruction that we encounter when trying to apply the finite element method in terrain-following coordinates.
Put a different way, conventional DG schemes are designed to recover continuity of $u$ in the limit as the mesh is refined.
Here we use a discontinuous Galerkin-like method to instead recover continuity of $Ju$.
Note that we can use either continuous or discontinuous basis functions for the velocity.
Indeed, many of our unit tests will use Taylor-Hood elements for the velocity and pressure.
As long as we (1) use the contravariant components of the velocity field in the curvilinear coordinate system and (2) discretize the thickness field with finite elements, we need to include the facet integrals that are normally used with discontinuous basis functions.
But if we are willing to accept the added complexity of DG facet terms, then we may as well use a DG basis.

The source code to produce the free energy rate for the Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates with both conforming and non-conforming elements is shown in Figure \ref{fig:source-code-tfc}.
The function \texttt{free\_energy\_rate\_facets} computes the facet corrections to the free energy rate that are associated with discontinuous elements.
We add this facet correction for all elements (continuous and discontinuous) when the thickness is discretized.

The final missing piece is what to do about slip boundary conditions.
We can again proceed by a rote application of the transformation rules to the extra terms in equation \eqref{eq:nitsche-slip}.
For the no-penetration penalty, the algebra works out in such a way that the factors of $|nJ^{-1}|$ all cancel out.
For the flux term, the algebra does not work out as cleanly.
These terms translate to:
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        G_\alpha(u, p) = & \ldots - \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\left(\frac{n\cdot J^{-1}\tau J^{-*}n}{|nJ^{-1}|^2} - p\right)u\cdot n\; h\ud\gamma \label{eq:robin-tfc-flux}\\
        & \qquad + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu|\gamma|}{2|\omega|}(u\cdot n)^2\, h\ud\gamma \\
        & \qquad\quad + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\kappa}{2}\left|\left(I - \frac{nJ^{-1}\otimes nJ^{-1}}{|nJ^{-1}|^2}\right)u\right|^2|nJ^{-1}|\,h\ud\gamma \label{eq:robin-tfc-slip}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here it is simpler to specialize to horizontal and vertical facets.
Define $\nu = (-\sigma^*, 1)$ to be the normal vector to horizontal facets.
Then
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
        \text{equation \eqref{eq:robin-tfc-flux}} & = -\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_H\cap\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\left(\frac{\nu^*\tau\nu}{|\nu|^2} - p\right)u\cdot n\,h\ud\gamma + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_V\cap\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\left(n^*\tau n - p\right)u\cdot n\,h\ud\gamma \\
        \text{equation \eqref{eq:robin-tfc-slip}} & = \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_H\cap\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\kappa}{2}\left|\left(I - \frac{\nu\otimes\nu}{|\nu|^2}\right)u\right|^2|\nu|h\ud\gamma + \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_V\cap\Gamma_R}\int_\gamma\frac{\kappa}{2}\left|(I - n\otimes n)u\right|^2h\ud\gamma
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
Specializing to horizontal and vertical facets simplifies out several terms that look like $h / h$.
Moreover, slip boundary conditions are usually only applied at the ice base and therefore on horizontal facets.
We can then leave off any terms for slip boundary conditions on vertical facets.

\section{Implementation}

We use the finite element modeling package Firedrake to discretize the problem \citep{rathgeber2016firedrake}.
A valuable feature for simulating thin-film flows is the ability to create tensor product meshes and elements.
The kernels for assembling stiffness matrices and load vectors can be optimized specifically for tensor product meshes in a way that is not available on general unstructured meshes \citep{mcrae2016automated}.
In all test cases, we use LU factorization in order to be sure that any errors are due to the discretization and not to a poorly-tuned iterative solver.
The penalty parameters $\alpha$ in the SIPDG methods are all chosen according to the explicit lower bound from \citep{warburton2003constants}.

We use the Irksome package for timestepping \citep{farrell2021irksome}.
The Unified Form Language used in Firedrake and FEniCS was designed only to express the spatial part of a partial differential equation, leaving the end user to implement timestepping.
Irksome extends the Unified Form Language with time derivatives in order to express evolution equations.
From this specification, Irksome then generates all of the low-level code to implement implicit Runge-Kutta methods.
These schemes are often considered too difficult to implement for practical use.
As we argue below, Irksome makes using higher-order implicit schemes no more difficult than using backward Euler.
The problems that we consider here are all dissipative, so we focus on the Radau-IIA family of methods, which are absolutely stable.

    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{demo/testing/flowchart.pdf}
    \caption{Flowchart of test suite.}
\end{figure}

There are a lot of moving parts in the derivations above.
We have designed a battery of tests to catch potential failures of either the math or the code.
We describe each of our tests and the kinds of errors that they caught below.
At a high level, we first solving the Stokes equations on a fixed geometry and then test solving the Stokes equations with a time-evolving geometry.

The ideal test case would use an analytical solution.
With a variable topography, however, there are few useful closed-form solutions.
We settled on first constructing a ``ground truth'' solution which can be presumed correct.
For example, the highest-level ground truth solution is obtained by solving the Stokes equations in Cartesian coordinates on a mesh with variable topography using Taylor-Hood elements.
The key code to express the variational form is three lines of Python code using Firedrake.
It has fewer potential error modes than, say, using a discontinuous Galerkin discretization, terrain-following coordinates, or both.
We can then compare the alternative solutions to the numerical ground truth.

\subsection{Static problems}

The tests on static problems are broken down in a fine-grained way because we need to get several things correct and all of them are error-prone.
For example, our goal at this stage is to solve the Stokes problem in terrain-following coordinates where the thickness and bed topography are discretized in a finite element space.
Suppose that the numerical solution we obtain does not map onto the corresponding solution in Cartesian coordinates.
The variational form requires DG facet terms, even when we use conforming elements for velocity and pressure, because of the discontinuity in the matrix $J$ described above.
It's not obvious right away whether this discrepancy is because of a mistake in the facet integrals or in the derivatives of the coordinate transformation.
We can pinpoint the source of this failure by instead first testing in isolation (1) a DG method in Cartesian coordinates against a conforming method and (2) a conforming method in terrain-following coordinates where the thickness and bed elevation are described exactly through their analytical expressions rather than discretized in a finite element space.

\paragraph{DG discretization in Cartesian coordinates.}
Our first test is to isolate whether the implementation of the interior penalty DG method is correct on a Cartesian mesh, without the complication of a curvilinear coordinate system.
We solve the Stokes equations in Cartesian coordinates with variable surface and bed topography using Taylor-Hood elements.
Then we use non-conforming elements and the symmetric interior-penalty method.
If the norm difference between the two solutions does not go to zero as the mesh is refined, then the most likely explanation is that either the math or the implementation of the interior penalty method is incorrect.
Implementing the symmetric interior penalty method is notoriously error-prone \citep{houston2018automatic}.
A typical error in implementing a DG discretization would be making a sign error in the facet flux term.

\paragraph{Conforming discretization in exact terrain-following coordinates.}
Our next test is to evaluate whether we have implemented terrain-following coordinates correctly, assuming that we have an analytical expression for the fluid thickness and the bed elevation.
This assumption lets us sidestep the issues associated with discretizing these fields in a finite element space.
Here we can use Taylor-Hood, MINI, or other conforming elements for the velocity and pressure with no DG correction terms.
If we map the solution in terrain-following coordinates back to Cartesian coordinates and obtain a vector field that differs from what we obtained in the previous steps, then either the math or the implementation is wrong.
This stage of testing caught errors in the translation of math to code.
In our first pass, we missed a factor of $h$ in one of the terms of the variational form and inserted an extra factor of $J$ in the force term.

\paragraph{Non-conforming discretization in exact terrain-following coordinates.}
A non-conforming discretization using the symmetric interior penalty method introduces additional terms into the variational form.
Specifically, these are facet integrals that force the solutions toward continuity and boundary integrals.
A common mistake in implementing the symmetric interior penalty method is mixing up the sign of the facet integrals, but this is largely caught and debugged by the implementation in Cartesian coordinates.
Another common mistake is forgetting factors of $h$, i.e. the Jacobian of the transformation from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates, in the cell integrals.
This mistake will have been caught in testing the implemenation of a conforming discretization in Cartesian coordinates.

The new mistake that is likely to appear only at this stage is getting the factors of the Jacobian or its inverse correct in the facet integrals.
The velocity field transforms as a vector and so it picks up a factor of $J$ in transforming to terrain-following coordinates.
But the unit outward-pointing normal vector is not a vector but a covector, and so it transforms by multiplying on the right by $J^{-1}$.
During our first pass at implementing terrain-following coordinates with a non-conforming discretization, we made the mistake of thinking of the unit outward-pointing normal as a vector rather than a covector.
The numerical solutions remained a positive distance apart from the conforming solution in the limit of mesh refinement.
The more interesting feature to note here is that these are mistakes in understanding the math rather than mistakes in translating math to code.

\paragraph{Conforming discretization in discrete terrain-following coordinates.}
A parallel but related test to using a non-conforming discretization is what happens when the bed elevation $b$ and fluid thickness $h$ are not known exactly but instead are discretized with continuous finite elements.
The derivative $J$ of the transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates, defined explicitly in equation \eqref{eq:dx-dxi}, is now discontinuous across cell boundaries.
This matrix $J$ appears under a spatial derivative in the expression in equation \eqref{eq:velocity-gradient} for the velocity gradient.
Even if we use Taylor-Hood or MINI elements for the velocity and pressure, we need to include the facet integrals that are normally used only for non-conforming methods in order to correctly discretize the problem when $b$ and $h$ are discretized.

This stage of testing has the same error modes as implementing a non-conforming discretization with an exact geometry.
Discretizing $h$ introduces an additional failure mode not in the specification of the Stokes problem itself but in how to compare a solution in terrain-following coordinates with one in Cartesian coordinates.
The conversion between the two requires multiplication by the matrix $J$, but this matrix now lives in a DG space.
A naive implementation would take the converted velocity to use the same polynomial degree as the original terrain-following velocity.
This solution will compare poorly with the Cartesian solution, not because it was wrong but because the degree needs to be the original degree \emph{plus} the degree of the thickness space.

\paragraph{Non-conforming discretization in discrete terrain-following coordinates.}
This is the final stage before testing time-dependent problems and includes all of the features we desire for our discretization of the momentum balance equation.
This test was successful on the first pass because all of the mistakes had already been made in the previous two stages.


\subsection{Time-evolving problems}

The next step is to test problems where the thickness can evolve in time and finally also the bed.
Again, there are several respects in which we can isolate potential failure modes.
The discontinuity in $J$ with Lagrange finite elements is a major potential failure mode.
We can temporarily work around this problem by using $C^1$ elements for the bed elevation and thickness.
These elements are respectively the Hermite element in 1D and Argyris or Hsieh-Clough-Tocher in 2D.
The time-evolving solution obtained with these elements can then serve as the ground truth against which we compare the solutions obtained with Lagrange elements.

\subsection{Relaxation of sinusoidal surface}

\begin{figure}[t]

    \end{center}
    \caption{Relaxation of sinusoidal initial surface.
    Contours show the surface every 2 years of simulation time.}
    \label{fig:relaxation}
\end{figure}

As a ``smoke test'', we consider relaxation of a sinusoidal surface based on the first test case from \citet{kramer2012implicit}.
We used a no-slip condition on the bottom of the domain, free-slip conditions on the side walls, and no stress on the surface.
The parameters for the simulation are described in \textcolor{red}{Table X}.
For the numerical scheme, we use DQ${}_2$ elements for the velocity, DQ${}_1$ elements for the pressure, and CG${}_1$ elements for the thickness.
We used a mesh with 50 elements in the horizontal and 5 in the vertical.

The simulation runs for 20 years.
First we compare a splitting scheme with an implicit scheme.
We implement the splitting scheme by lagging the thickness in the momentum balance equation.
The velocity is calculated using the thickness from the previous timestep, while the thickness is updated using this newly-computed velocity.
The alternative is a fully-implicit backward Euler scheme where we simultaneously solve the mass and momentum balance equations.
The splitting scheme is only stable for timesteps smaller than 1/16 year.
The implicit scheme remains stable for all timesteps, even up to 20 years.
Figure \ref{fig:relaxation} shows the evolution of the fluid surface with a timestep of 1/2 year.
With these timesteps, the splitting scheme takes 10 times longer to run to completion than the fully implicit scheme.
The implicit scheme with a timestep of 1/2 year required 2 Newton iterations for every step.
When we increased the timestep to 20 years, the simulation required only one additional Newton iteration to converge.

Next, we tested the Radau-IIA(2) timestepping scheme.
The backward Euler method is the lowest-order instance of the Radau-IIA family.
The Radau-IIA schemes are collocation methods that are of maximal order subject to the constraint that the right endpoint of the interval is a collocation point.
The k-stage Radau-IIA scheme is of order $2k - 1$ and its stability region contains the entire left half of the complex plane, i.e. it is A-stable.
We tested the Radau-IIA scheme with timesteps of 1 year.
The first step required 3 Newton iterations and every subsequent timestep required 2.
Like backward Euler, the Radau-IIA(2) also converged in 3 Newton iterations with a timestep of 20 years.

Finally, we ran a simulation with double the number of elements in both directions.
If the number of the number of Newton iterations increases as the mesh is refined, then the method might fail to be scalable.
We found that every step required 2 Newton iterations on the refined mesh as well.

\subsection{Perlin glacier}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{demo/perlin-glacier/perlin-glacier.png}
    \end{center}
    \caption{Evolution of a glacier over randomized bed topography every 10 years over a 400-year spin-up.}
    \label{fig:perlin-glacier}
\end{figure}

For a more involved test case, we use a variant of the ``Perlin glacier'' problem from \citet{ahlkrona2026fully}.
Our focus here is on the use of terrain-following coordinates and discontinuous Galerkin methods, so instead of using the Glen flow law, we use a linear viscous rheology.
The sliding law is the same.

The bed topography is the sum of a baseline which is a quadratic function of the distance and a perturbation:
\begin{equation}
    b = \alpha/L\cdot(x - L)^2 + \delta b.
\end{equation}
The perturbation is a sum of random cubic splines, each of which is divided into ``octaves'':
\begin{equation}
    \delta b = \sum_{k = 1}^K c_k\,\delta B_k(x)
\end{equation}
where $c_k$ is the amplitude of the $k$th octave.
Each octave constituent $\delta B_k$ is a cubic spline with $2^k$ knot points distributed evenly through the interval.
The values of $\delta B_k$ are set to 0 the derivatives are chose uniformly in the interval $[-1, 1]$.
We used $K = 4$ octaves with amplitudes $[1/4, 1/2, 1, 1]$.

The original test case in \citet{ahlkrona2026fully} uses a surface mass balance that is a function of distance and fixed for all time.
We added feedback between the surface elevation and surface mass balance:
\begin{equation}
    \dot a = \max\left\{\dot a_{\text{max}}, \frac{\delta a}{\delta s}(s - f\alpha L)\right\}
\end{equation}
where the dimensionless fraction $f$ dictates where the equilibrium line altitue (ELA) is relative to the top of the mountain and $\delta a/\delta s$ is tuned in order to make the accumulation equal to its maximum value at the top of the mountain.
Here we took the maximum accumulation rate to be $\dot a_{\text{max}} = 1$ m/yr and the ELA fraction $f = 0.4$ so that the ELA occurs at a height of 320 m.
We then spin the up the model for 400 years.

We use again DQ${}_2$ elements for velocity, DQ${}_1$ elements for pressure, and CG${}_1$ elements for thickness.
The mesh had 500 elements in the horizontal and 5 in the vertical.
We used a timestep of 1 year.
We used an absolute convergence tolerance of $10^{-10}$ and a relative tolerance of $10^{-8}$.
The results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:perlin-glacier}.
With these parameters, nearly all timesteps required 3 to 4 Newton iterations and only a handful required 5.

We tested several other configurations against this default setup.
We ran the same simulation with 10 vertical elements instead of 5.
Several common Stokes elements, including Taylor-Hood, have a stability constant that is bounded away from zero only if the aspect ratio of the mesh is bounded.
Thin-film flows often violate this assumption so it is desirable to have elements that remain stable even in the limit of small aspect ratio.
We found that the distribution of Newton iterations necessary to achieve convergence was the same in this case as for the default.
Second, we increased the polynomial degree to DQ${}_3$ for the velocity and DQ${}_2$ for the pressure.
Again, the number of Newton iterations was roughly the same as the default case.
Finally, we used the 2-stage Radau-IIA scheme.
Here we found that the number of Newton iterations was consistently higher than for backward Euler, with a maximum of 9.
It is possible that the number of iterations could be reduced by first computing a backward Euler solution and then linearly interpolating to the Radau-IIA collocation points in the interior.
The relative differences in the fluid thickness and terminus position in steady state between the configurations are less than 1/100.

\section{Discussion}

We presented a reformulation of the Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates and showed how to discretize the problem with the discontinuous Galerkin finite element method.
This approach goes beyond existing work in the literature on computational fluid dynamics and offers a few advantages.
The most significant advantages are overcoming the drunken sailor instability and providing a clear path to applying higher-order timestepping schemes.

Several previous works have used terrain-following coordinates for ice flow modeling.
\citet{kleiner2014numerical} is the closest in spirit to this work.
They used a higher-order ice flow model with a transformed coordinate system but discretized the problem using the finite difference method.
\citet{greve2009dynamics} describe the the implementation of the ice-flow model SICOPOLIS, which also uses a finite difference discretization in terrain-following coordinates but focuses more on the shallow ice approximation.
Our approach differs from both of these in that (1) we worked with the contravariant components of the velocity vector, (2) we used the full Stokes equations, and (3) we used the finite element method rather than the finite difference method.

The velocity vector has an existence independent of the reference frame, but to do computations we have to write it out in components.
In a curvilinear coordinate system there are several choices to make about which components \citep{yang1994general}.
We elected to work with the contravariant components of the velocity vector in the curvilinear coordinate system rather than the Cartesian velocities as is done in e.g. SICOPOLIS.
\citet{greve2009dynamics} state in \S5.7.2:
\begin{displayquote}
    Note that, while applying the sigma transformation, we have not attempted to write the velocity vector $\mathbf v$ in components of the curvilinear, transformed coordinates.
    Instead, the Cartesian components $u = v_x$ and $w = v_z$ are treated like scalar fields which are not affected by the transformation.
    This is common practise in connection with the sigma transformation; otherwise, the transformed equations would become very complicated due to the non-orthogonality of the transformed coordinates.
\end{displayquote}
The transformed equations that we use do have extra factors of $J$.
We had to use special techniques in order to discretize the problem because of these extra terms.
The equations would indeed become too complicated to implement if we were to write out every component of the stress tensor and the momentum balance equation.
But we were able to avoid a great deal of messy algebra by working at the level of vector and tensor fields rather than their individual components.

We argue that working directly with the contravariant components of the velocity in the non-orthogonal coordinate system also has advantages.
The main benefit is in prescribing the top and bottom boundary conditions.
The Cartesian vertical component of the ice velocity at the surface or bed of a glacier does not completely prescribe the mass balance rate.
Instead, we need to also know the surface and bed slope and the other components of the velocity vector.
In contravariant terrain-following coordinates, however, the vertical component of the velocity times the thickness is exactly equal to the mass balance rate.
Implementing the normal-flow boundary condition in Cartesian coordinates involves additional work.
For example, the Elmer/ICE package used an ad hoc procedure to define the unit outward normal vector at the mesh vertices \citep{gagliardini2013capabilities} before implementing another approach based on Nitsche's method \citep{cheng2020full}.
Implementing boundary conditions correctly is one of the most common sources of error in numerical discretization of differential equations.
We argue that using the contravariant components of the velocity more directly corresponds with physical intuition about the vertical velocity and makes errors in the boundary conditions less likely.

Where both \citet{greve2009dynamics} and \citet{kleiner2014numerical} use a finite difference discretization, we used a discontinuous Galerkin finite element method.
Implementing the finite element method from scratch is arguably more involved than the finite difference method.
New software tools such as Firedrake and UFL significantly reduce the gap in complexity.
The modeler only has to correctly prescribe a variational form rather than write low-level computational kernels for finite element assembly.

Using terrain-following coordinates provides an easy path toward applying implicit timestepping schemes, including higher-order methods like Radau-IIA.
We showed in the sinusoidal relaxation problem that implicit methods are not as susceptible to the drunken sailor instability.
We regard this feature as one of the major benefits of terrain-following coordinates.
\citet{ahlkrona2026fully} also used fully-implicit schemes for free-surface Stokes flow.
They solved the nonlinear system of equations for the velocity and the surface through a nonlinear Gauss-Seidel iteration.
This scheme alternates between the Stokes and free-surface solves until convergence.
They used this strategy to implement both a backward differentiation formula and the Crank-Nicholson scheme.
A critical element of this approach was the addition of an augmented free-surface stabilization term, which they derive by hand through Taylor expansion of the discrete problem, over and above the usual stabilization.
This extra stabilization (equation (19) of \citet{ahlkrona2026fully}) helps to guarantee that the surface sub-step of the Gauss-Seidel iteration remains stable even for long timesteps.
On the other hand, we were able to apply a higher-order timestepping scheme ``out of the box'' with no extra terms in the variational form.

Several papers in computational fluid dynamics have used Riemannian geometry to describe flow in evolving curved geometries, for example \citet{luo2004contravariant}.
Here the outlook is just as bad as for curvilinear coordinates discretized with finite elements.
We need derivatives of the Riemannian metric (the Christoffel symbols) in order to calculate the covariant derivative of the velocity.
The Riemannian metric, like the deformation matrix $J$, is discontinuous across cell boundaries if we discretize with finite elements.
We chose to avoid Riemannian geometry for two reasons.
First, the language is highly technical and will be unfamiliar to many earth scientists.
Second, as shown above, we can use DG schemes to confront the continuity problems associated to curvilinear coordinates.
A handful of papers have derived the Navier-Stokes equations in time-dependent curvilinear coordinates without Christoffel symbols.
\citet{warsi1981conservation} derived both conservation and differential forms of the Navier-Stokes equations in curvilinear coordinates in a way that avoids Christoffel symbols.
\citet{yang1994general} built on this to simulate fluid flow in curved geometries using the finite volume method.
We are not aware of any papers that looked at a variational form or discretization via finite elements.

There are several problems that this work does not address: nonlinearity, divergence-conforming or anisotropic finite elements, and solver strategies.

First, we focus here on linear problems.
Many earth materials are plastic, pseudoplastic, or have some other non-Newtonian constitutive law.
A straightforward extension of equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc} to nonlinear constitutive relations may or may not be possible depending on the form of the non-linearity.

Second, we focused here on conventional families of discontinuous finite elements.
There is a middle ground where one instead uses div-conforming bases like Raviart-Thomas or Brezzi-Douglas-Marini.
These bases guarantee exact element-wise mass conservation.
The best that other bases can guarantee is mass conservation in an average sense \citep{cockburn2007note}.
The catch when using equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc} is that the divergence operator now includes a factor of $h$.
Obtaining divergence-conformity with a different divergence operator would require significant modification.
One possibility would be working with $hu$ as the main unknown instead of $u$, but we would then need to rework the form of the equations.
Additionally, the stability and convergence theory for most finite element bases assumes that the cells of the mesh are shape-regular.
Certain choices of finite element basis still work well even when the mesh is highly anisotropic.
We did some testing of how well a standard discretization performed with a smaller aspect ratio but did not take this to extremes.
These techniques will be much more effective on typical problems in the earth sciences if they can be made robust to extremely small aspect ratio.

Finally, we used standard Newton methods with a line search and direct factorization of the associated linear systems.
Nonlinear preconditioning could significantly reduce the cost burden of higher-order timestepping schemes.
The simplest approach would be to interpolate an initial backward Euler solution as a first guess at the values of a Radau-IIA solution at other collocation points.

These issues and others will be the subject of future work.

\section{Three-field form}

Here we show how and why to discretize the Stokes problem in curvilinear coordinates where the stress tensor is an additional unknown.
The three-field form of the Stokes problem has a minimization principle which we can obtain by applying the principle of convex duality to equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz}:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-xyz}
    \begin{align}
        G_\alpha^*(u, p, \tau) = & \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{1}{4\mu}|\tau|^2 - \tau :\dot\varepsilon(u) + p\nabla\cdot u + f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm d\omega \\
        & \qquad + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma \left\langle\tau - pI \right\rangle: [u\otimes n]\,\mathrm d\gamma \\
        & \qquad\qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu|\gamma|}{2|\omega|}|[u\otimes n]|^2\mathrm d\gamma
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
There are two features to note.
In equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz}, the stress tensor $\tau$ is to be substituted with $2\mu\dot\varepsilon(u)$.
In the previous equation, $\tau$ is another independent variable and its relationship to the strain rate tensor is enforced in a variational sense.
Second, the sign on the facet fluxes and penalty has changed.
While the primal form in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz} is a saddle-point problem, the sign flip makes the dual form of equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-xyz} a \emph{double-saddle} problem \citep{boffi2013mixed}.
Each problem type demands different solution strategies.

The form in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-xyz} assumes that the stresses are in $L^2(\Omega, \mathbb S)$ and the velocities in $H^1(\Omega, \mathbb{R}^d)$.
This form is more common in the literature on implicit constitutive relations.
In general it is more common to look for a stress in $H(\text{div})$ and a velocity in $L^2$.
The reason to prefer a stress in $H(\text{div})$ is because it can give a more accurate approximation of the stress at the expense of a less accurate approximation for the velocity for the same number of degrees-of-freedom.
For some applications that tradeoff is desirable.

We can get the three-field form of the Stokes problem in terrain-following coordinates through a rote application of the transformation rules to equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-xyz}:
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-tfc}
    \begin{align}
        G_\alpha^*(u, p, \tau) & = \sum_\omega\int_\omega\left\{\frac{1}{4\mu}h|\tau|^2 - h\tau:\dot\varepsilon + p\nabla\cdot(h u) + hf\cdot J^*Ju\right\}\ud\omega \\
        & \qquad + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\left\{\left\langle\tau\right\rangle:[Ju\otimes n\,J^{-1}] + \langle p\rangle[u\cdot n] \right\}\,h\ud\gamma \\
        & \quad\qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu|\gamma|}{2|\omega|}|[Ju\otimes nJ^{-1}]|^2\,h\ud\gamma \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-tfc-penalty}
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
The same considerations, such as the double-saddle structure, apply as in the Cartesian case.

Why would we want to use the dual form if it's more expensive?
The usual reason to include the stress as an unknown is because the constitutive law is defined implicitly \citep{farrell2020numerical}.
This paper is concerned only with Newtonian fluids, but the three-field form has advantages even for Newtonian fluids when using terrain-following coordinates.
In the primal form of equation \eqref{eq:stokes-tfc}, there is an unavoidable factor of the inverse of $h$.
The first term consists of the product of $\dot\varepsilon(u)$ with itself.
Recall from equation \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc} that each factor of the strain rate carries a factor of $J^{-1}$, which in turn carries a factor of $h^{-1}$.
We multiply this first term by $h$ and that cancels out one factor of $h^{-1}$, but it still leaves the other.
Now by passing to the dual form in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-tfc}, there is now only one factor of $\dot\varepsilon(u)$.
That term is multiplied by $h$.
The advantage of having only factors of $h$ and no factors of $h^{-1}$ is that the integrand is a polynomial.
We can then pick a quadrature rule that allows us to integrate it exactly, ruling out a potential source of error.
The expense of including the stress as an unknown might not be a good price to pay just to turn a rational integrand into a polynomial.
Still, the difference is worth pointing out, and the simplification can be more valuable when considering nonlinear rheologies.

The overall dissipation rate is still not a polynomial because there are two factors of $J^{-1}$ and only one counterbalancing factor of $h$ in the penalty term in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-dual-tfc-penalty}.
We can reduce the whole functional to a polynomial through hybridization: introduce a Lagrange multiplier to enforce continuity across facets.

It is worth noting that the duality pairing in terrain-following coordinates is not the standard $L^2$ inner product but rather a weighted inner product with a factor of $h$:
\begin{equation}
    \langle \tau, u\rangle = \int_\Omega h\tau : \dot\varepsilon(u)\,\mathrm d\omega.
\end{equation}
Trying to apply convex duality directly to equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-tfc} with the wrong inner product results in a less tractable dissipation rate.
