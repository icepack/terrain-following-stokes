\section{Introduction}

Here we describe discretization schemes for viscous free-surface fluid flows.
In atmospheric science and oceanography, using terrain-following coordinates is a common approach for handling variable surface and bottom topography.
For these problems, viscous dissipation is negligible except in a very thin boundary layer.
As a consequence, the equations of motion contain only first-order derivatives in space and time.
Here we are interested in other free-surface fluid flows in the earth sciences, such as the flow of glaciers or lava.
For these types of flows, viscous effects are dominant and the inertial terms are negligble.
The equations of motion contain second-order derivatives.
This makes it much harder to apply terrain-following coordinates because the metric terms are discontinuous and live inside a derivative.
Here we show how to work around this difficulty using discontinuous Galerkin methods.

Using interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin methods requires setting certain penalty parameters.
Many papers in the research literature assume that this constant is big enough without deriving how big that is.
An essential tool for us will be the explicit estimates for constants in the finite element trace inverse inequality of \citet{warburton2003constants}.
This will let us set the constants explicitly in terms of the mesh regularity.

We assume that the fluid flow takes place over a variable topography with elevation $b$.
The fluid surface is at height $s$ and the film thickness is $h = s - b$.

\section{DG discretization of Stokes}

The incompressible Stokes equations can be derived through minimization of the free energy dissipation rate
\begin{equation}
    G(u, p) = \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau :\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot u - f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm dx
\end{equation}
where $\dot\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla u + \nabla u^*)$ is the rate of strain tensor and $\tau = 2\mu\dot\varepsilon$ is the deviatoric stress tensor.
We will use the minimization form in the following because it requires fewer characters to type.

\begin{lstlisting}
import firedrake
from firedrake import inner, outer, sym, grad, div, dx, ds, dS, avg, jump


def stress_strain_rate(u):
    ε = sym(grad(u))
    return ε, 2 * μ * ε


def free_energy_rate(u, p, f):
    ε, τ = stress_strain_rate(u)
    return (0.5 * inner(τ, ε) - p * div(u) - inner(f, u)) * dx


def free_energy_rate_dg(u, p, f, α):

    u_n = outer(u, n)

The description above requires boundary conditions to be complete.
We will concern ourselves with two types of boundary conditions.
First, we can prescribe the boundary traction by adding a term to the free energy rate:
\begin{equation}
    \ldots - \int_\Gamma \tau_\Gamma : n\otimes u\,\mathrm d\gamma.
\end{equation}
We might instead want to fix the value of the velocity.
The conventional approach is to modify the linear system at the time of solution to enforce this Dirichlet condition.

Nitsche's method is a way to instead enforce Dirichlet conditions by modifying the variational form.
One way of looking at Nitsche's method is as an augmented Lagrangian scheme together with an exact solution for the Lagrange multipliers.
The Nitsche functional is
\begin{equation}
    G_\alpha(u, p) = G(u, p) - \int_\Gamma(\tau - pI) : n\otimes(u - u_\Gamma)\mathrm d\gamma + \int_\Gamma\frac{\alpha\mu}{2\ell}|u - u_\Gamma|^2\mathrm d\gamma.
\end{equation}
Here $\ell$ is a measure of the local facet diameter and $\alpha$ is a constant related to the mesh regularity.

The symmetric interior penalty discontinuous Galerkin method is what happens when we apply Nitsche's method to every cell $\omega$ of the mesh:
\begin{align}
    & G_\alpha(u, p) = \sum_\omega\int_\omega\left\{\frac{1}{2}\tau:\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot u - f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm dx \nonumber \\
    & \qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma \left\langle\tau - pI \right\rangle: [u\otimes n]\,\mathrm d\gamma + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\frac{\alpha\mu}{2|\gamma|}|[u]|^2\mathrm d\gamma \label{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz}
\end{align}
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denotes the average across a face and $[\cdot]$ denotes the jump.
To be completely explicit, the jump of the velocity is
\begin{equation}
    [u\otimes n] = u_+\otimes n_+ + u_-\otimes n_-
\end{equation}
where the ``$+$'' and ``$-$'' denote each side of the facet $\gamma$ that the jump is evaluated on.
We can catch potential errors in the variational form by remembering that the DG facet fluxes should be the same if we swap which side of a facet is the ``$+$'' side and which is the ``$-$'' side.
With interior penalty methods, we can use finite element bases that do not meet the continuity requirements of the Stokes equations.
Figure \ref{fig:source-code-xyz} shows the source code for producing the free energy rate in Cartesian coordinates for both conforming and non-conforming elements.
The same discretization can be derived from a conservation law using the local discontinuous Galerkin method \citep{cockburn2002local}.

The interior penalty method makes it possible to use completely discontinuous elements for the velocity.
It is also possible to use elements that are intermediate between continuous and discontinuous.
An \emph{$H(\text{div})$-conforming} element has continuous normal components across mesh facets but has no constraints on continuity tangential to mesh facets \citep{boffi2013mixed}.
The interior penalty method then recovers continuity in the tangential direction in the limit as the mesh is refined.
The advantage of this intermediate continuity is that it implies that the divergence operator exactly maps the velocity space into the pressure space.
We are then assured that mass is conserved exactly and not approximately \citep{cockburn2007note}.

In the test cases below, we used two families of elements, both on quadrilaterals in 2D.
To obtain the conforming, ground truth solution, we used Taylor-Hood elements.
We then used a non-conforming discretization where the velocity is in $H(\text{div})$.
Here we choose the Brezzi-Douglas-Marini element of degreee $k + 1$ for the velocity and discontinuous polynomials of total degree $k$ for the pressure.
In Firedrake or UFL parlance, these elements are respectively \texttt{BDMCF} and \texttt{DPC}.
Alternatively, we could have chosen Raviart-Thomas elements of degree $k + 1$ for the velocity and discontinuous tensor product polynomials of degree $k$ in each direction (\texttt{RTCF} and \texttt{DQ} respectively) but the Brezzi-Douglas-Marini space is slightly smaller for the same order of accuracy.
\textcolor{red}{Check if BDM handles anisotropy better than RT}.

We can also consider a formulation of the Stokes problem where the stress tensor is an additional unknown.
Although more expensive, this form will be indispensible for simulating non-Newtonian fluid flows.
There are several equivalent mixed forms of the Stokes problem.
Here we use the deviatoric stress tensor; it is also possible to use the full stress tensor.
The optimization form of this problem is to find a critical point of the functional
\begin{equation}
    L^*(u, p, \tau) = \int_\Omega\left\{\frac{1}{4\mu}|\tau|^2 - \tau :\dot\varepsilon(u) + p\nabla\cdot u + f\cdot u\right\}\mathrm dx.
\end{equation}
This form of the problem assumes that the stresses are in $L^2(\Omega, \mathbb S)$ and the velocities in $H^1(\Omega, \mathbb{R}^d)$.
It is more common to look for a stress in $H(\text{div})$ and a velocity in $L^2$.
We will use a non-conforming discretization in the end, so these distinctions will become unimportant.

\section{Terrain-following coordinates}

A typical introductory continuum mechanics textbook starts by describing Eulerian (fixed in space and time) and Lagrangian (material-following) reference frames.
For the most part, it is more common to use a fixed than a moving reference frame for computational fluid dynamics.
The simplest explanation for this preference is that, for many interesting fluid flows, an initially circular representative volume element can deform into one with a much larger surface-to-volume ratio.
For many problems, the system is open and so using a material-following coordinate system introduces additional complications at the inflow and outflow boundaries.

For free surface flows, the geometry is changing in time and there are choices the modeler has to make about how to capture this effect.
One possibility is to use a fixed coordinate system and a moving mesh.
The ice-flow modeling pacakge Elmer/ICE uses a Cartesian coordinate system and advects the mesh with the flow using an arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian scheme \citep{gagliardini2013capabilities}.
Alternatively, one can use a fixed mesh and a moving coordinate system.
The package SICOPOLIS uses terrain-following coordinates, described in \S5.7 of \citet{greve2009dynamics} and below.
Finally, one can use a completely fixed mesh and coordinate system and use interface-tracking techniques such as the level-set method \citep{hossain2020modelling}.
\textcolor{red}{Check ISSM, PISM, BISICLES if we want to be exhaustive}.
Here we will pursue the approach of using a coordinate system that is fixed in the horizontal but moving in the vertical.


\subsection{Transforming vectors and gradients}

A moving free surface presents an additional challenge because the geometry of the problem is changing in time.
We can solve this problem -- while creating a host of other problems -- by transforming the coordinates.
The idea of terrain-following coordinates is to map the lower surface to 0 and the upper surface to 1.
In so doing, we can work on the Cartesian product of a footprint domain $\Phi$ and the unit interval $[0, 1]$.
We will denote the Cartesian coordinates as $x_1, x_2, x_3$ and terrain-following coordinate as $\xi_1, \xi_2, \xi_3$.
The transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates is
\begin{equation}
    \left[\begin{matrix}x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3\end{matrix}\right] = \left[\begin{matrix}\xi_1 \\ \xi_2 \\ b(\xi_1, \xi_2) + \xi_3h(\xi_1, \xi_2)\end{matrix}\right].
\end{equation}
The complications come in because the surfaces of constant $\xi_3$ are curved.
Taking a derivative with respect to the horizontal coordinates with the elevation $x_3$ fixed is not the same as taking a derivative with $\xi_3$ fixed.
We'll show below how to transform the Stokes equations into this new coordinate system.

The explicit expression for the transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates will let us also transform vector and tensor fields and their derivatives.
First, we'll introduce a vector field
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \nabla b + \xi_3\nabla h
    \label{eq:slope-field}
\end{equation}
describing the slope in physical space of surfaces of constant $\xi_3$.
The vector field $\sigma$ interpolates between the slope of the bed and the slope of the surface.
In the expression for the slope field, we don't have to specify whether we are taking the gradient in one coordinate system or the other because $b$ and $h$ only depend on the horizontal coordinates.
Then we can concisely express the derivative of the transformation from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates as
\begin{equation}
    J = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\xi} = \left[\begin{matrix}I & 0 \\ \sigma^* & h\end{matrix}\right].
    \label{eq:dx-dxi}
\end{equation}
We now know how to transform integrals into terrain-following coordinates:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm dx = |\det J|\mathrm d\xi = h\,\mathrm d\xi.
\end{equation}
As a sanity check, this weighting factor makes the physical units work out correctly.

Now we also have to think about how to transform vectors and tensors between coordinate systems.
The velocities in Cartesian and terrain-following coordinates are not the same.
We can derive the relation between them using the chain rule, and reasoning informally that a velocity is a rate of change of position with time:
\begin{equation}
    u_x = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\xi}\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dt} = Ju_\xi.
\end{equation}
The body forces transform the same way as well:
\begin{equation}
    f_x = Jf_\xi.
\end{equation}
Intuitively, the gravitational acceleration vector is perpendicular to surfaces of constant depth but is not perpendicular to surfaces of constant relative depth.
We can see from the explicit expression for $J$ in equation \eqref{eq:dx-dxi} that, because of the identity block, the only difference is in the vertical components of the velocity and force.

Next we need to be able to transform derivatives.
To do that, we'll need to know what the derivative of the inverse transformation back from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates is.
We could compute the inverse transformation and then differentiate it, or we could use the implicit function theorem and the fact that $J$ is a triangular matrix.
These yield the explicit expression
\begin{equation}
    J^{-1} = \frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx} = \left[\begin{matrix}I & 0 \\ -h^{-1}\sigma^* & h^{-1}\end{matrix}\right].
    \label{eq:dxi-dx}
\end{equation}
Given an arbitrary field $\phi$, the chain rule tells us that
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dx} = \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm d\xi}\cdot\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dx} = \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm d\xi}\cdot J^{-1}.
\end{equation}
This is just a restatement of the fact that derivatives transform as covectors and not as vectors.
We can then write down an expression for the velocity gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_xu_x = \nabla_\xi(Ju_\xi)J^{-1}.
    \label{eq:velocity-gradient}
\end{equation}
We obtain the strain rate tensor by symmetrizing this expression.

Finally, we need to compute the divergence of a vector field.
In Cartesian coordinates, the divergence operator is the (negative) adjoint of the gradient operator:
\begin{equation}
    \int_\Omega q\nabla_x \cdot v_x\,\mathrm dx = -\int_\Omega\nabla_x q\cdot v_x\,\mathrm dx.
\end{equation}
The general expression for the divergence in other coordinate systems follows by requiring this relationship to remain true.
We know how $\mathrm dx$, $v_x$, and $\nabla_x q$ transform from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates:
\begin{align}
    \ldots & = -\int_{\Phi \times [0, 1]}\nabla_\xi q \,J^{-1}\cdot Ju_\xi\;h\,\mathrm d\xi \nonumber\\
    & = -\int_{\Phi\times[0, 1]}\nabla_\xi q\cdot h u_\xi\;\mathrm d\xi \nonumber\\
    & = \int_{\Phi\times[0, 1]}q\nabla_\xi\cdot(hu_\xi)\,\mathrm d\xi.
\end{align}
Here we used the fact that the factors of $J^{-1}$ and $J$ cancel out, then applied the usual trick to move the gradient back over as a divergence.
Now we can assert that defining
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x\cdot u_x = h^{-1}\nabla_\xi\cdot (hu_\xi)
\end{equation}
preserves the relationship between the divergence of a vector field and the gradient of a scalar field.

\subsection{The Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates}

Now we have all of the ingredients that we need to express the minimization form of the Stokes problem in terrain-following coordinates:
\begin{equation}
    G(u, p) = \int_\Phi\int_0^1\left\{\frac{1}{2}h\tau :\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot(hu) - hf\cdot J^*J u\right\}\mathrm d\xi
    \label{eq:stokes-tfc}
\end{equation}
where the deviatoric stress $\tau$ is again equal to $2\mu\dot\varepsilon$, but the rate of strain tensor is
\begin{equation}
    \dot\varepsilon = \frac{1}{2}\left\{\nabla(Ju)J^{-1} + J^{-*}\nabla (Ju)^*\right\}.
    \label{eq:strain-rate-tfc}
\end{equation}
While there are extra terms in this functional compared to the expression in Cartesian coordinates, all of them are expressible in the Unified Form Language or related domain-specific languages for PDEs.

Finally, the thickness evolves in time according to a conservative advection equation.
The variational form of this equation is
\begin{equation}
    \int_\Phi\left\{\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}\phi - h\bar u\cdot\nabla\phi\right\}\mathrm dx = \int_\Phi(\dot a - \dot m)\phi\ud x
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is an arbitrary test function, $\bar u$ is the depth-averaged velocity, and $\dot a$, $\dot m$ are the accumulation and ablation rates.
This equation requires some modification when we discretize, which we describe below.

\subsection{Riemannian geometry (or not)}

A reader who knows Riemannian geometry will recognize that the metric tensor $J^*J$ has made its entrance in the inner product between the body force and the velocity.
So far we have avoided explicitly using Riemannian geometry.
There are two reasons for this choice.
First, many earth scientists will be unfamiliar with the terminology.
Second, and more to the point, suppose that the bed elevation and thickness have been discretized using continuous Galerkin finite elements.
Then the slope field $\sigma$ in equation \eqref{eq:slope-field} is discontinuous in the horizontal direction.
Consequently, \textbf{both the deformation matrix $J$ and the metric tensor are discontinuous}.
In order to compute covariant derivatives using the usual formulas from Riemannian geometry, we need to calculate the notorious Christoffel symbols.
But the Christoffel symbols are derivatives of the metric tensor.
We could treat the Christoffel symbols as distributions.
If we could do that, the discretization would be more complicated.
But there is a convincing reason to believe that we cannot do that in the first place.
There is no well-defined concept of the product of distributions in classic distribution theory.
We need to (for example) take products of the strain rate tensor with itself in order to compute the dissipation rate, and that would involve products of distributions.

We have instead avoided metrics, Christoffel symbols, or Riemannian geometry.
It's possible to approach the problem this way because we start with an explicit isometry into Euclidean space.
A handful of other works in the research literature have derived the Navier-Stokes equations in time-dependent curvilinear coordinates without Christoffel symbols for either or both of the reasons we described above.
\citet{warsi1981conservation} derived both conservation and differential forms of the Navier-Stokes equations in curvilinear coordinates in a way that avoids Christoffel symbols.
\citet{yang1994general} built on this to simulate fluid flow in curved geometries using the finite volume method.
We are not aware of any research works examining a variational form or discretization via finite elements.

\section{Discretization in terrain-following coordinates}

    J_inv = coordinate_transformation_derivative_inverse(b, h)

    J_u = dot(J, u)
    ε, τ = stress_strain_rate(u, b, h)

Suppose that we discretize the bed elevation $b$ and thickness $h$ using a standard continuous finite element basis.
The derivative $J$ of the coordinate transformation involves gradients of $b$ and $h$.
That means that $J$ is discontinuous across cell boundaries.
But if we examine expression \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc}, $J$ appears under a gradient and we are faced with the problem of how to differentiate a discontinuous quantity.
This lack of continuity is the major problem we need to overcome.
We can solve that problem with the interior penalty DG method.
At a practical level, this amounts to substituting the transformed expressions for $u$ and its derivatives in terrain-following coordinates into the functional in equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz}.
For the integrals over cells $\omega$, this transformation is straightforward and already contained in equations \eqref{eq:stokes-tfc} and \eqref{eq:strain-rate-tfc}.
The major change is in the facet integrals.
Here there is one subtlety that can easily lead to mistakes.
The velocity $u$ transforms as a vector, but the unit outward-pointing normal $n$ instead transforms as a covector.
The easiest way to remember how $n$ transforms is to recall that it is the gradient of the signed distance function to the boundary of the domain.
We can then more properly think of $n$ as a row vector which should be right-multiplied by $J^{-1}$.
The analogue of equation \eqref{eq:sipdg-stokes-xyz} in terrain-following coordinates is then
\begin{align}
    G_\alpha(u, p) & = \sum_\omega\int_\omega\int_0^1\left\{\frac{1}{2}h\tau:\dot\varepsilon - p\nabla\cdot(h u) - hf\cdot J^*Ju\right\}\ud\zeta \mathrm d\xi \nonumber \\
    & \qquad - \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\int_0^1\left\{\left\langle h\tau\right\rangle:[Ju\otimes n\,J^{-1}] + \langle p\rangle[hu\cdot n] \right\}\ud\zeta\ud\gamma \\
    & \quad\qquad + \sum_\gamma\int_\gamma\int_0^1\frac{\alpha\mu}{2|\gamma|}|[Ju]|^2\, \langle h\rangle\ud\zeta\ud\gamma.
\end{align}
Even if we used the Taylor-Hood or MINI velocity-pressure pair, we would still need the facet integrals in order to account for the discontinuity in $J$ when the bed elevation and thickness are discretized in a finite element space.

In Cartesian coordinates, we can use $H(\text{div})$-conforming elements like Brezzi-Douglas-Marini for the velocity in order to obtain better mass conservation properties and pressure robustness.
These elements not fully discontinuous; instead, they have continuous normal components.
This property guarantees that the divergence of the discrete velocity space is a subspace of the pressure space.
It is a good question to ask whether we can preserve this property in a general curvilinear coordinate system, where the divergence operator picks up a factor of the determinant of $J$.
In our case, since we use continuous finite elements for $h$, and $h$ depends only on the horizontal coordinates and not the vertical coordinate, an $H(\text{div})$-conforming element for the velocity still retains all of its desirable properties even with a different divergence operator.

The source code to produce the free energy rate for the Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates with both conforming and non-conforming elements is shown in Figure \ref{fig:source-code-tfc}.
The function \texttt{free\_energy\_rate\_tfc\_dg}, which adds the corrections to the free energy rate that are usually associated with non-conforming elements, is used also for conforming elements when the thickness is discretized.

\section{Testing the implementation}

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{demo/testing/flowchart.pdf}
    \caption{Flowchart of test suite.}
\end{figure}

There are a lot of moving parts in the derivations above.
We have designed a battery of tests to catch potential failures of either the math or the code.
We describe each of our tests and the kinds of errors that they caught below.
At a high level, we first solving the Stokes equations on a fixed geometry and then test solving the Stokes equations with a time-evolving geometry.

The ideal test case would use an analytical solution.
With a variable topography, however, there are few useful closed-form solutions.
We settled on first constructing a ``ground truth'' solution which can be presumed correct.
For example, the highest-level ground truth solution is obtained by solving the Stokes equations in Cartesian coordinates on a mesh with variable topography using Taylor-Hood elements.
The key code to express the variational form is three lines of Python code using Firedrake.
It has fewer potential error modes than, say, using a discontinuous Galerkin discretization, terrain-following coordinates, or both.
We can then compare the alternative solutions to the numerical ground truth.

\subsection{Static problems}

The tests on static problems are broken down in a fine-grained way because we need to get several things correct and all of them are error-prone.
For example, our goal at this stage is to solve the Stokes problem in terrain-following coordinates where the thickness and bed topography are discretized in a finite element space.
Suppose that the numerical solution we obtain does not map onto the corresponding solution in Cartesian coordinates.
The variational form requires DG facet terms, even when we use conforming elements for velocity and pressure, because of the discontinuity in the matrix $J$ described above.
It's not obvious right away whether this discrepancy is because of a mistake in the facet integrals or in the derivatives of the coordinate transformation.
We can pinpoint the source of this failure by instead first testing in isolation (1) a DG method in Cartesian coordinates against a conforming method and (2) a conforming method in terrain-following coordinates where the thickness and bed elevation are described exactly through their analytical expressions rather than discretized in a finite element space.

\paragraph{DG discretization in Cartesian coordinates.}
Our first test is to isolate whether the implementation of the interior penalty DG method is correct on a Cartesian mesh, without the complication of a curvilinear coordinate system.
We solve the Stokes equations in Cartesian coordinates with variable surface and bed topography using Taylor-Hood elements.
Then we use non-conforming elements and the symmetric interior-penalty method.
If the norm difference between the two solutions does not go to zero as the mesh is refined, then the most likely explanation is that either the math or the implementation of the interior penalty method is incorrect.
Implementing the symmetric interior penalty method is notoriously error-prone \citep{houston2018automatic}.
A typical error in implementing a DG discretization would be making a sign error in the facet flux term.

\paragraph{Conforming discretization in exact terrain-following coordinates.}
Our next test is to evaluate whether we have implemented terrain-following coordinates correctly, assuming that we have an analytical expression for the fluid thickness and the bed elevation.
This assumption lets us sidestep the issues associated with discretizing these fields in a finite element space.
Here we can use Taylor-Hood, MINI, or other conforming elements for the velocity and pressure with no DG correction terms.
If we map the solution in terrain-following coordinates back to Cartesian coordinates and obtain a vector field that differs from what we obtained in the previous steps, then either the math or the implementation is wrong.
This stage of testing caught errors in the translation of math to code.
In our first pass, we missed a factor of $h$ in one of the terms of the variational form and inserted an extra factor of $J$ in the force term.

\paragraph{Non-conforming discretization in exact terrain-following coordinates.}
A non-conforming discretization using the symmetric interior penalty method introduces additional terms into the variational form.
Specifically, these are facet integrals that force the solutions toward continuity and boundary integrals.
A common mistake in implementing the symmetric interior penalty method is mixing up the sign of the facet integrals, but this is largely caught and debugged by the implementation in Cartesian coordinates.
Another common mistake is forgetting factors of $h$, i.e. the Jacobian of the transformation from Cartesian to terrain-following coordinates, in the cell integrals.
This mistake will have been caught in testing the implemenation of a conforming discretization in Cartesian coordinates.

The new mistake that is likely to appear only at this stage is getting the factors of the Jacobian or its inverse correct in the facet integrals.
The velocity field transforms as a vector and so it picks up a factor of $J$ in transforming to terrain-following coordinates.
But the unit outward-pointing normal vector is not a vector but a covector, and so it transforms by multiplying on the right by $J^{-1}$.
During our first pass at implementing terrain-following coordinates with a non-conforming discretization, we made the mistake of thinking of the unit outward-pointing normal as a vector rather than a covector.
The numerical solutions remained a positive distance apart from the conforming solution in the limit of mesh refinement.
The more interesting feature to note here is that these are mistakes in understanding the math rather than mistakes in translating math to code.

\paragraph{Conforming discretization in discrete terrain-following coordinates.}
A parallel but related test to using a non-conforming discretization is what happens when the bed elevation $b$ and fluid thickness $h$ are not known exactly but instead are discretized with continuous finite elements.
The derivative $J$ of the transformation from terrain-following to Cartesian coordinates, defined explicitly in equation \eqref{eq:dx-dxi}, is now discontinuous across cell boundaries.
This matrix $J$ appears under a spatial derivative in the expression in equation \eqref{eq:velocity-gradient} for the velocity gradient.
Even if we use Taylor-Hood or MINI elements for the velocity and pressure, we need to include the facet integrals that are normally used only for non-conforming methods in order to correctly discretize the problem when $b$ and $h$ are discretized.

This stage of testing has the same error modes as implementing a non-conforming discretization with an exact geometry.
Discretizing $h$ introduces an additional failure mode not in the specification of the Stokes problem itself but in how to compare a solution in terrain-following coordinates with one in Cartesian coordinates.
The conversion between the two requires multiplication by the matrix $J$, but this matrix now lives in a DG space.
A naive implementation would take the converted velocity to use the same polynomial degree as the original terrain-following velocity.
This solution will compare poorly with the Cartesian solution, not because it was wrong but because the degree needs to be the original degree \emph{plus} the degree of the thickness space.

\paragraph{Non-conforming discretization in discrete terrain-following coordinates.}
This is the final stage before testing time-dependent problems and includes all of the features we desire for our discretization of the momentum balance equation.
This test was successful on the first pass because all of the mistakes had already been made in the previous two stages.


\subsection{Time-evolving problems}

The next step is to test problems where the thickness can evolve in time and finally also the bed.
Again, there are several respects in which we can isolate potential failure modes.
The discontinuity in $J$ with Lagrange finite elements is a major potential failure mode.
We can temporarily work around this problem by using $C^1$ elements for the bed elevation and thickness.
These elements are respectively the Hermite element in 1D and Argyris or Hsieh-Clough-Tocher in 2D.
The time-evolving solution obtained with these elements can then serve as the ground truth against which we compare the solutions obtained with Lagrange elements.

\section{Discussion}

We presented an approach to formulating the Stokes equations in terrain-following coordinates and discretized the problem with the discontinuous Galerkin finite element method.
Several previous works have used terrain-following coordinates for ice flow modeling.
\citet{kleiner2014numerical} is the closest in spirit to this work.
They used a higher-order ice flow model with a transformed coordinate system but discretized the problem using the finite difference method.
\citet{greve2009dynamics} describe the the implementation of the ice-flow model SICOPOLIS, which also uses a finite difference discretization in terrain-following coordinates but focuses more on the shallow ice approximation.
Our approach differs from both of these in a few respects.

First, we elected to work with the contravariant components of the velocity vector in the curvilinear coordinate system rather than the Cartesian velocities.
\citet{greve2009dynamics} state in \S5.7.2:
\begin{displayquote}
    Note that, while applying the sigma transformation, we have not attempted to write the velocity vector $\mathbf v$ in components of the curvilinear, transformed coordinates.
    Instead, the Cartesian components $u = v_x$ and $w = v_z$ are treated like scalar fields which are not affected by the transformation.
    This is common practise in connection with the sigma transformation; otherwise, the transformed equations would become very complicated due to the non-orthogonality of the transformed coordinates.
\end{displayquote}
The transformed equations that we use do have extra factors of $J$.
We had to use special techniques in order to discretize the problem because of these extra terms.
We argue that working directly with the contravariant components of the velocity in the non-orthogonal coordinate system has advantages.
The main advantage is in prescribing the top and bottom boundary conditions.
The Cartesian vertical component of the ice velocity at the surface or bed of a glacier does not completely prescribe the mass balance rate.
Instead, we need to also know the surface and bed slope and the other components of the velocity vector.
In contravariant terrain-following coordinates, however, the vertical component of the velocity times the thickness is exactly equal to the mass balance rate.
Moreover, the horizontal contravariant components of the velocity are exactly equal to the sliding velocity and can thus be used directly to implement the sliding boundary condition.
Implementing sliding boundary conditions in Cartesian coordinates involves additional work.
For example, the Elmer/ICE package uses an ad hoc procedure to define the unit outward normal vector at the mesh vertices \citep{gagliardini2013capabilities}.
Alternatively, one could use Nitsche's method.
Implementing boundary conditions correctly is one of the most common sources of error in numerical discretization of differential equations.
We argue that using the contravariant components of the velocity more directly corresponds with physical intuition about the vertical velocity and makes errors in the boundary conditions less likely.

Second, where both \citet{greve2009dynamics} and \citet{kleiner2014numerical} use a finite difference discretization, we used a discontinuous Galerkin finite element method.
Implementing the finite element method from scratch is arguably more involved than the finite difference method.
New software tools such as Firedrake and UFL significantly reduce the gap in complexity.
The modeler only has to correctly prescribe a variational form rather than write low-level computational kernels for finite element assembly.
The use of $H(\text{div})$-conforming elements for the ice velocity make it possible to exactly conserve mass, which is more difficult to attain with finite difference methods.
